# Teacher guide — Wordscape Arena

Prepared for Year 9 descriptive writing and connotation practice.

## Run a lesson

1. Create a room and keep “I’m hosting only” selected if you want to supervise without a score. Display the host screen on your classroom screen.
2. Ask students to join on their own devices. Room codes are not passwords; share the code with your class.
3. Before starting, remind students to think about appearance, texture, light, space, movement and feeling.
4. Start the 90-second round. Let students discover vocabulary independently; do not display a player’s solved answers to the class.
5. At the review, compare two words that create different impressions. Ask students to point to a detail that supports their choice.
6. Use the model sentence as a starting point, then have students revise the atmosphere by changing two adjectives.
7. Continue at the class’s pace. The eight guessing rounds take 12 minutes; allow extra time for discussion. Download scores at the end if wanted.

## Important teaching decisions

- A missed guess can still be a valid descriptor. The automatic checker only recognises the curated targets and their listed spelling variants. Avoid presenting a non-match as bad writing.
- Points reflect vocabulary challenge. A high-point word is useful only when its meaning fits the scene and the intended mood.
- “Positive”, “negative”, “neutral” and “mixed” describe plausible connotations in these scenes. They are not permanent rules for every use of a word.
- Some words, such as “cacophonous” or “scorching”, suggest a sound or condition rather than prove it. Ask students to frame them as an imaginative interpretation supported by the picture.
- All players can score the same word once. Another player’s speed does not remove learning opportunities.
- If a student has a brief connection problem, ask them to refresh the same tab. Their score resumes while that server session remains active.

## Suggested extension

After the game, students choose one photo and write 80–120 words. Require three precise descriptive choices, one deliberate change of mood, and a short explanation of how one adjective shapes the reader’s response. Reward specificity and control rather than piling up adjectives.
